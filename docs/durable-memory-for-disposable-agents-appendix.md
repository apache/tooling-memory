# Appendix G: Evidence

Appendix G of [*Durable Memory for Disposable Agents*](durable-memory-for-disposable-agents.md), kept in its own file because it changes with the research more often than the design does. Sources were read on 2 November 2026, the practice sources of §13's industry row ([116] to [140]) on 3 November 2026, and the sources first read as abstracts or not opened on 4 November 2026, in full text unless marked "abstract only".

## Purpose, and how to read it

The paper states each property of the design once, with at most a citation or two, and keeps its reasoning short. This appendix lists every property the paper commits to (a principle, a rule, a mechanism, a number) and says, for each, why it is there, what evidence stands behind it, how strong that evidence is, and what observation would make the paper revise or drop it. It exists so that when new research arrives, a reader can find the row it bears on and judge the paper's position without re-deriving it.

Terms such as T0, the historian, the curator, a tap, and the §11 arms are defined in the paper; [Appendix F](durable-memory-for-disposable-agents.md#appendix-f-glossary) is its glossary.

Each row links to the heading in the paper where the property is stated. Citations use the paper's reference numbers and link to its reference list. A quotation or number appears only where the source's full text was read and the number was found there with its conditions (benchmark, models, sample size, mean or maximum). Where only an abstract could be read, the cell says "abstract only" and the row is not rated above Single study on that source. Where the source could not be opened, the cell says so, and confidence is judged from the source's standing. Where a property rests on the paper's own reasoning, the cell says that plainly. Several sources may support one property; where two confidence levels apply, the stronger is given first and the other is mentioned. The last column, "Measured by", names what would show the property holding, or paying, in a deployment: an **Arm** of the §11 evaluation; a §9 **Metric** or other telemetry the service already records; a **Suite** (golden, recall, held-out, seeded-fault, or red-team); a **Replay** of logged decisions; a **Drill** the operator runs on a cadence; a **Test** of the service that a forbidden operation is refused or a required one happens; an **Audit**, a query over the historian or the registry; or **Unmeasured**, where the row is a stance, a definition, or provenance. Where a row names two kinds, the first is the primary one.

## The confidence scale

| Level | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Established** | Long-standing practice or standards with wide adoption, such as leases, event sourcing, or partial evaluation in a policy engine |
| **Replicated** | Measured by two or more independent groups |
| **Single study** | One paper or report, however good. Single-author work and a group's own benchmark are noted |
| **Incident** | Grounded in a documented real-world incident |
| **Vendor report** | A vendor's own account of its own system |
| **Argued** | Rests on reasoning in the paper alone, without external evidence |

The table lists the levels from strongest to weakest, and this order holds throughout the appendix. The levels rate the evidence alone. A rule that is Argued may be sound; it is simply unmeasured. The section "Where the design is most exposed" ranks properties by the cost of being wrong, so the paper's exposure is visible in one place.

| Property | Why it is there | Evidence | Confidence | What would change it | Measured by |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| [Everything is partitioned into domains with an owner; memory, tasks, policy, and access all follow the domain](durable-memory-for-disposable-agents.md#2-the-design-at-a-glance) | One boundary for data, access, ownership, and conflict | Bounded contexts [[50]](durable-memory-for-disposable-agents.md#ref-50) (book, not opened); data mesh [[51]](durable-memory-for-disposable-agents.md#ref-51) opened: "Domain Bounded Context is a wonderfully powerful tool to design the ownership of the datasets" | Established | Telemetry in a deployment showing most reads cross domain boundaries, so the boundary costs more than it saves; the paper's own remedy is to redraw it | **Metric**: share of reads and of request tasks that cross a domain boundary; unmatched tasks per domain |
| [Agents are stateless and disposable; anything worth keeping is written before the agent ends](durable-memory-for-disposable-agents.md#principles) | Makes the agent cheap to start and safe to lose, and puts all continuity in the record | [[109]](durable-memory-for-disposable-agents.md#ref-109) (full post): long-running agent sessions begin "with no memory of what came before" and carry state in a progress file and git history, because "compaction isn't sufficient"; no measurements. [[110]](durable-memory-for-disposable-agents.md#ref-110) (full text; chat tasks without agents): spreading instructions over turns cut performance 39% on average across 15 models; one consolidated restatement reached 95.1% of the fully specified baseline. [[111]](durable-memory-for-disposable-agents.md#ref-111) (abstract only): errors fell 23% after a structured handoff programme in nine hospitals; not randomised, and the programme included training and observation. No study compares an agent's handoff note directly with transcript replay. Convergent practice: [[83]](durable-memory-for-disposable-agents.md#ref-83) | Single study; [109] vendor report | Measured quality or cost gains from long-lived agents with private state that a handoff note cannot match | **Arm**: transcript replay against full, and a seated session against a seatless one on the same work, paired by task |
| [Read the least that answers: index, then summary, then section, then document](durable-memory-for-disposable-agents.md#principles) | Reading is the dominant cost and overloaded contexts degrade output | Paper's own argument. [[18]](durable-memory-for-disposable-agents.md#ref-18) (full text, arXiv v2): a two-tier design in which the model pages data between its context and recall and archival stores by function call under memory-pressure warnings; its gains (deep memory retrieval 32.1% to 92.5% with GPT-4) are against baselines that saw only a lossy summary, and it reports no token or cost figures, so it is a precedent for tiering and no evidence on reading cost. [[56]](durable-memory-for-disposable-agents.md#ref-56) (full text, authors at mem0.ai): 1,764 context tokens per query against 26,031 for full context (about 93%); the graph variant saves about 86%; p95 latency 17.1 s to 1.44 s; the OpenAI baseline is non-standard. [[92]](durable-memory-for-disposable-agents.md#ref-92) on cost of context files | Single study ([92]); [56] vendor report; otherwise Argued | The §11 arms showing full-document loading beats summaries-first on success at acceptable cost in a domain; the paper already allows the per-domain verdict | **Arm**: full against whole-document loading; **Metric**: fetch-through rate, wasted reads, retrieval depth per task |
| [Coordinate through shared state, not agent-to-agent messaging](durable-memory-for-disposable-agents.md#principles) | Messaging grows with the square of the fleet; shared state gives one account of what is taken and decided | Blackboards [[45]](durable-memory-for-disposable-agents.md#ref-45) (full text, Computing Surveys 1980): knowledge sources "interact uniformly and anonymously via the blackboard", activated by others' blackboard actions "rather than explicit calls", for modularity and swappable modules; the shared state came with one central scheduler, and routing fine-grained steps through the blackboard caused "intolerable performance degradation"; one speaker, 23 utterances. Tuple spaces [[46]](durable-memory-for-disposable-agents.md#ref-46) (full text, TOPLAS 1985): tuples exist independently of sender and receiver and are withdrawn atomically, so of two contenders "one gets it and the other does not"; the argument is uncoupling and avoiding a concentrator process, and the author warns that "care must be taken to prevent unauthorized access to stored tuples"; a design with a four-node prototype and no efficiency result. Neither source argues from message volume; the square-of-the-fleet rationale is the paper's. Stigmergy [[47]](durable-memory-for-disposable-agents.md#ref-47) (not opened), [[48]](durable-memory-for-disposable-agents.md#ref-48): the 2016 paper was not opened; the author's 2011 working paper of the same material says work "leaves a trace in the environment that stimulates the performance of subsequent work". [[3]](durable-memory-for-disposable-agents.md#ref-3) shows agents choose shared state unprompted | Established | A measured fleet in which direct messaging coordinated better at scale than a ledger, with the audit trail intact | **Test**: no agent-to-agent channel exists in the service; **Metric**: dependency cycles, priority reversals and missed commitments |
| [Agents decide within fixed limits; a policy engine enforces them; only a person can widen permissions](durable-memory-for-disposable-agents.md#principles) | A model can be wrong or manipulated, so some limits must hold regardless | Separation of privilege [[31]](durable-memory-for-disposable-agents.md#ref-31) opened: a mechanism "that requires two keys to unlock it is more robust and flexible than one". [[38]](durable-memory-for-disposable-agents.md#ref-38) on checking actions outside the model | Established | Nothing measurable; this is a stance on where authority sits. A regulator or institution accepting agent-granted permission would not change the paper's position | **Test**: a grant widened without an approval event from a person is refused; **Audit**: approval events against the grants in force |
| [Everything stays human-legible; people are the only continuity at decade scale](durable-memory-for-disposable-agents.md#principles) | The final backstop when every tool is gone | Paper's own argument | Argued | Evidence that legibility costs more than it buys, for example a record too large for any person to use even in a text editor | **Drill**: the restore drill's reading of the estate by a person; **Metric**: review latency as a proxy for legibility. Otherwise unmeasured |
| [Evaluation is built in; every layer, memory included, can be switched off](durable-memory-for-disposable-agents.md#principles) | A component that cannot show it helps should not be kept | Paper's own argument; the arms are in §11. [[64]](durable-memory-for-disposable-agents.md#ref-64) shows why paired comparison is needed | Argued | None; this is a method. What changes is what the evaluations find | **Test**: every layer has an off switch and an arm differs from production in configuration only. The claim is a method; what it finds is in the §11 rows |
| [Components are deliberately common: Markdown, BM25, OPA, OpenTelemetry; vector search only as a derived layer](durable-memory-for-disposable-agents.md#principles) | Unusual dependencies become liabilities over decades | Paper's own argument. Convergence on Markdown with frontmatter: [[95]](durable-memory-for-disposable-agents.md#ref-95), [[96]](durable-memory-for-disposable-agents.md#ref-96) (vendor reports) | Argued | A component the paper names losing maintenance or governance; the paper's own record test and ownership test are the check | **Audit**: the record test and the ownership test, applied to each named component on a published cadence |
| [A seat is a place knowledge lives: a standing role record a session may boot into](durable-memory-for-disposable-agents.md#roles) | A session that starts from nothing re-derives who is asking, what is in scope, and what it owes on every start | One operator's account, Steve Yegge's Gas Town: [[117]](durable-memory-for-disposable-agents.md#ref-117) (full text) describes seats as named roles with persistent identity and history under disposable sessions, and the cost argument that caution one can look up is caution one can afford; [[120]](durable-memory-for-disposable-agents.md#ref-120) (full transcript) describes the same seats with charters, lanes, retirement plans, and forced handoff before context degrades. ## §4 Trust and handling

| Property | Why it is there | Evidence | Confidence | What would change it | Measured by |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

## Where the design is most exposed

Most of the design's structure is Argued: it follows from a few grounded premises (the incidents, trust non-transitivity, authority collapse, revocation failure, fast-falling prices) by reasoning the paper lays out but does not measure. That is expected of a design paper. The list below ranks properties by the cost of being wrong, the costliest first. Some are Argued; others rest on evidence that is thin or narrow.

1. **Agents are stateless and disposable; the handoff note carries all continuity.** Everything else assumes this. It has support from practice [109], from a study of consolidated restatements on chat tasks [110], and from hospital handoffs [111]. No study compares an agent's handoff note with transcript replay. If long-lived agents with private state did measurably better work than a chain of cold starts reading handoffs, the ledger, leases, and the write path would all need rethinking. Seats ([§8](durable-memory-for-disposable-agents.md#seats)) give a role the continuity that Steve Yegge built into Gas Town, one operator's fleet [117], [120], and keep it in shared memory, so the stance stands; whether a seated session does better than a seatless one reading the same record is the same open question. The §11 arms can test it, but no result exists yet.
2. **Four labels, never one score.** The design's trust model is its most distinctive part and the costliest to enforce. The evidence that composite scores fail ([8], and the laundering channels in [81]) is one study each, on synthetic benchmarks. A composite shown safe across poisoning, leakage, and revocation suites would simplify the whole of §4.
3. **Reading costs, and the discipline holds even if reading becomes free.** Three groups find accuracy falling as input grows with the evidence present [101]–[103], so the row is Replicated. It stays on this list because the size of the effect varies widely by model and task, and some current models show none on simple lookups. If that becomes general, the T0 cap, summaries-first retrieval, and the recall deputy survive on cost alone.
4. **Independent auditors on a different model where a capable one is available.** Self-preference [104] and failed self-correction [105] support a different model, and correlated errors [106]–[108] bound what it buys. Two small studies add that a weaker reviewer can make work worse [112] and that a fresh session on the same model finds about as many planted errors as the best cross-model reviewer, and partly different ones [113]. So the rule asks for a reviewer that audits at least as well as the curators' model, and falls back to a fresh session on the curators' model with more sampled human review. If same-model audit caught as many seeded faults, the design would keep auditors but lose the independence rule.
5. **Curation with a protected floor, and a summary rebuilt from its sources each time.** The premise, that updating a summary again and again, one batch at a time, loses detail, is Replicated. The remedy is Argued: no study has shown that rebuild-from-sources curation keeps memory above the no-memory floor over years. [85] found that static consolidation of a whole pool held at 100% while streaming updates degraded, which is closer to the paper's batched materializer than to its curator loop. The raw-only arm is the paper's own test, and it has not been run.
6. **One memory server per session.** Grounded in one researcher demonstration [89]. If protocols gained a way to enforce one filter across servers, the rule would soften to a preference.
7. **The horizon scanner is a person.** The design puts outward-looking judgment with a named human and gives no mechanism for it beyond a budget and a review. This is where the paper most openly declines to automate, and it has no evidence either way.
8. **Spend and throughput as two separate limits.** The reasoning is clear and the practice (quotas) is common, but no fleet has been shown to need both; if capacity becomes elastic the throughput limit may never bind.
